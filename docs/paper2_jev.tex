\begin{abstract}
LLM pipelines for applicant ranking typically combine free-text assessment with iterative listwise comparison, an approach that needs extra machinery against hallucinated claims and provides no built-in confidence signal. In September 2026, TypeSafe AI released Jev, the first ``System One Model'': a non-autoregressive architecture that answers typed questions in one parallel pass and reports a calibrated confidence per answer, removing free-text generation and its hallucination risk by construction. This research's objective is to propose two architecture-level mechanisms for reliably deploying Jev in a real applicant-ranking pipeline: criteria-grounded question design, and cost/latency efficiency from a single parallel pass outperforming an iterative tournament. The research method transforms an existing LLM-and-tournament applicant-ranking pipeline, stage by stage, into a fully structured typed-question architecture built on Jev and these two mechanisms, evaluated on 273 shortlisted (job, applicant) pairs across 10 job profiles. The results show that criteria-grounded design raises per-requirement answer confidence from 0.672 to 0.894 and pooled seniority/education confidence from 0.636 to 0.766; that single-call ranking convergence reaches 0.964, exceeding the prior tournament-based system's 0.957, using well under half as many model calls and roughly 6--10$\times$ lower per-candidate latency, with recommendation agreement holding steady across independent repeats. Overall, a non-autoregressive structured-decision model can outperform free-text assessment in reliability, provided its question design and aggregation match its architecture.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{R}{ecruitment} platforms increasingly use large language models (LLMs) to read resumes, summarize applicant fit, and compare applicants against a job profile, in place of manual screening or keyword search \cite{ref1,ref2,ref3,ref4}. These systems are useful for the same reason they are risky: they generate free text that can assert a strength or weakness the source resume never supports. Prior work mitigates this with prompt-level constraints, retry-on-contradiction loops, post-hoc auditing \cite{ref2}, and iterative listwise tournaments with Plackett-Luce aggregation to turn per-applicant judgments into a defensible ranking \cite{ref1,ref2}. Both exist because next-token sampling over an open vocabulary gives no structural guarantee against an ungrounded claim, and no native signal of the model's confidence.

This paper asks whether answering predefined, typed questions instead of generating open text can outperform free-text assessment without reintroducing the reliability problems it is meant to solve. Two gaps motivate this study: first, no independent, peer-reviewed evaluation of System One Models exists yet -- Jev's release \cite{ref11} appeared only days before this paper -- so every reliability claim currently comes from TypeSafe-controlled or community sources \cite{ref11,ref17,ref18,ref19,ref20}; second, the recent literature on LLM calibration and self-consistency \cite{ref5,ref6,ref7} targets autoregressive judges that produce a scalar score or a free-text verdict, and none addresses a model whose fundamental outputs are a bounded probability (Noul), a categorical distribution (Choice), or an ordinal distribution (Score) answered in one parallel pass, so its diagnostics and mitigations do not transfer directly.

Beyond the primitive specifications, TypeSafe and early adopters document implementation guidance largely absent from the academic literature on structured-output LLMs: decomposing a multi-factor judgment into one field per factor and combining them deterministically in code, rather than asking the model for a single blended judgment \cite{ref17,ref18}; a documented aggregation pattern that normalizes each Score to its $[0,1]$ position before combining fields \cite{ref17,ref18}; and a generic harness that routes each typed decision to act automatically, route for human review, or escalate to a reasoning model, framed along six suitability dimensions -- judgment call, bounded, atomic, context-contained, faster than a human, machine-consumed \cite{ref17}. This paper's design choices (Section III) follow these principles directly.

This paper makes the following two architecture-level contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Criteria-Grounded Question Design}: the first large-sample statistical test of TypeSafe's own criteria-grounded Score-question guidance, extended to seniority and education Score questions, each validated with concrete-situation criteria in place of bare ordinal labels.
\item \textbf{Cost and Latency Efficiency}: a direct sort on Jev's per-call composite score outperforming the prior system's iterative listwise tournament -- no iterative ranking process, all calls parallelize, total model calls cut to well under half, and per-candidate wall-clock latency lower by roughly 6--10$\times$.
\end{enumerate}

The pipeline also includes a must-have/nice-to-have skill classification used by the shortlisting stage, its necessity validated by a diagnostic; it is described in Section III as part of the pipeline's stage-by-stage methodology rather than as a third contribution.

\section{Literature Review}

The system this paper extends combines LLM-driven free-text assessment with an active listwise tournament aggregated via Bayesian Plackett-Luce under Monte Carlo knowledge-gradient subset selection \cite{ref1,ref2}. Other systems apply LLMs to resume extraction and pointwise scoring inside retrieval-augmented or multi-agent architectures, without a comparative ranking step \cite{ref3,ref4}. None of this work departs from free text or free-form JSON as its output mode, so all of it inherits the hallucination-mitigation burden \cite{ref2} addresses with a retry loop, and none reports a native confidence that could distinguish a well-supported judgment from a guess.

A separate literature asks whether an LLM's stated confidence reflects its true correctness probability. Yaldiz et al.~\cite{ref5} find RL from verifiable rewards (RLVR) improves accuracy but makes decision-making LLMs overconfident. Wang et al.~\cite{ref6} find structured-output reliability varies sharply across models and prompts under a fixed schema. Haldar and Hockenmaier~\cite{ref7} document substantial self-inconsistency in LLM-as-a-judge frameworks, mitigated by averaging judgments; a survey by Gu et al.\ catalogs reliability strategies for such judges, but every one surveyed still targets a free-text or scalar-score judge \cite{ref14}. All of it treats the model as autoregressive, estimating confidence from token probabilities or resampling -- an assumption Jev's natively-trained, non-autoregressive confidence field does not share, so these findings cannot be assumed to transfer.

Jev is explicitly not a conventional language model: TypeSafe's own documentation states plainly that ``Jev is not a language model. You give it a text and typed questions, and it answers each one with a confidence. It does not write text'' \cite{ref20}. Mechanically, it replaces autoregressive sampling with a parallel sampler: for every typed question, the possible outputs are enumerated in advance and scored in one pass over the state, so schema conformance is guaranteed by construction rather than achieved by sampling and validating -- no tokens are generated, and output is priced at zero accordingly \cite{ref11}. Every question in a call is answered independently against the same state, so adding a question to a call costs negligibly more than the call alone \cite{ref11}.

The three typed primitives differ in shape, not just label. \emph{Noul} returns a single calibrated value in $[0,1]$, the probability of ``yes''; there is no separate confidence field, since the probability itself is the whole answer. \emph{Choice} supports up to 255 mutually exclusive options, with confidence computed from how concentrated the returned probability mass is across them. \emph{Score} supports 2--10 ordinal levels and returns a probability-weighted mean position, a confidence derived the same way, and the full per-level probability vector; for every primitive, reported confidence is a derived margin from a decision threshold, not a raw output probability \cite{ref20}. Critically, each Score level is scored independently -- the model is not shown the level's number or its neighbors -- so a level distinguished only by an implicit numeric position (e.g., ``50'' next to ``75'') carries no information the model can act on, while a level described as a concrete situation gives it resume evidence to match against directly. This is the mechanistic basis, not just an empirical correlation, for TypeSafe's own recommendation to write Score-question criteria as concrete situations rather than bare ordinal labels.

TypeSafe calls this a ``System One Model,'' trained with a proprietary method it names Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD), which optimizes for calibration directly rather than for a human-preference reward (RLHF) or a programmatically verifiable outcome reward (RLVR -- the paradigm Yaldiz et al.\ \cite{ref5} show becomes overconfident even as it grows more accurate) \cite{ref11}. Calibration is a narrower guarantee than correctness: as TypeSafe's own community documentation puts it, ``calibration does not mean that an individual prediction is guaranteed to be correct'' \cite{ref18} -- it means reported confidence tracks the true hit rate across many predictions, which is what the confidence-based diagnostics used throughout this pipeline (Section III) rely on. The underlying architecture (encoder-only, decoder-only, or otherwise) is undisclosed in every source consulted, including TypeSafe's own. As used here, it accepts up to 64{,}000 tokens per request (32{,}000 for the state plus the longest question), with reported accuracy degrading as irrelevant context is added to the state.

Stage 1 (extraction) and Stage 2 (shortlisting) are unchanged in kind from the prior system \cite{ref2}, since neither generates free text. Skill/job profile extraction runs throughout on a conventional autoregressive LLM (Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct), unlike Jev.

\subsection{Stage 1: Ingestion and Skill/Job Profile Extraction}
Job profiles and resumes are ingested as plain text, and an autoregressive LLM (Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct) extracts a normalized skill list, each technical skill additionally classified as must-have or nice-to-have for use by shortlisting (Section III-C). This stage does not use Jev and is not one of this paper's two contributions; its output feeds Stage 3.

\subsection{Stage 2: Shortlisting}
An applicant is shortlisted only if enough extracted skills match the job profile's by cosine similarity above a fixed threshold, and enough of those matches are must-have skills (thresholds in Table~\ref{tab_config}); requiring must-have coverage, not only a total count, keeps a shortlisted candidate from being admitted almost entirely on nice-to-have skills. Section III-F validates this requirement against a total-only baseline. Shortlisting, like extraction, does not use Jev.

\subsection{Stage 3: Structured Assessment via Jev (Criteria-Grounded Question Design)}
Where the prior system's free text lived, the pipeline sends one call to Jev per shortlisted pair (C1). Each call gives Jev one state (job profile, extracted skills, resume text) and typed questions answered in one pass: a hiring-recommendation Choice, a minimum-qualifications Noul, one Score per technical requirement, one Score per stated seniority or education requirement, and one Noul per stated certification -- one judgment per field, combined afterward. Each answer is retained in full, including its per-level (Score) or per-option (Choice) probability distribution alongside the collapsed value and confidence, so downstream stages can use either representation.

Match-degree questions use Score, not Choice, since a match degree is a position on a spectrum. Every Score question's five levels (within TypeSafe's documented 2--10 range) are written as concrete situations rather than bare ordinal labels, exploiting the per-level independence described in Section II: e.g., a requirement ``mentioned only as a bare skill-list item'' versus one ``used substantively in a real role or project with clear responsibility.'' The same design applies to education; the seniority question, asked only when the job profile states an explicit minimum, is a single objective years-of-experience match kept separate from the subjective per-requirement fit judgment. Section V-A validates this design at scale against a bare ordinal-number (0/25/50/75/100) baseline.

A composite fit score is then computed following TypeSafe's documented aggregation pattern (Section I): each Score normalized to its $[0,1]$ position, and the composite is the unweighted mean of every technical-requirement, seniority, and education Score in the call.

\subsection{Stage 4: Ranking}
Stage 3 (Section III-D) yields one composite score per (job, applicant) pair; applicants are ranked by a direct sort on this score. This direct sort outperforms the prior system's iterative Monte Carlo knowledge-gradient tournament with Bayesian Plackett-Luce aggregation: no ranking process, no waiting on a prior iteration's result. Because Jev's calls need no iterative dependency, they also parallelize, unlike the prior system's tournament (C2); Section V-C quantifies the resulting reduction in model calls and wall-clock latency. The ranked output then feeds a diagnostic check (Section III-F) that reads per-requirement scores afterward for upstream errors.

\subsection{Stage 5: Score Breakdown Diagnostic}
Because Jev reports a separate score per extracted requirement rather than a single free-text verdict, a job profile's shortlisted pool can be inspected at the level of individual skills, not only an aggregate score. A requirement is informative only if the pool's median score on it is reasonably high; a shortlisted applicant scoring near-zero on most informative requirements is flagged -- not because Jev judged them harshly, but because peers could clear a bar this applicant could not. Applied under a total-only shortlisting baseline (Stage 2's cosine threshold without the must-have gate), this diagnostic quantifies the must-have gate's necessity; Section V-B reports the corpus-wide result. This diagnostic validates Stage 2's must-have gate; it is not one of this paper's two architecture-level contributions.

\subsection{Must-Have Gate: Diagnostic Validation}
Applied to the full corpus, the must-have gate (Section III-C) excludes 44 of 303 candidates (14.5\%) that a total-only baseline (Section III-F) would shortlist: candidates who matched enough skills in total but not enough must-have skills. The gate is driven by classifying each job profile's technical skills as must-have or nice-to-have during extraction, of which 39\% were classified must-have. This gate is a pipeline design validated using C1's per-requirement granularity (Section III-F), not one of this paper's two architecture-level contributions.

Repeating the repeatability and ranking-convergence evaluation on the must-have-gated pool shows the gate and the seniority question do not degrade Jev's core reliability properties: recommendation agreement across repeats remains comparable, composite-score repeat stdev is essentially unchanged, and single-call ranking convergence is only slightly lower -- consistent with restriction of range: excluding candidates on nice-to-have-skill grounds leaves a more uniformly-qualified pool, in which similarly-qualified candidates are intrinsically harder to rank apart consistently. The criteria-grounding findings hold, reported in Table~\ref{tab_score_ablation} above, which is itself computed on this same must-have-gated pool.

Across the repeatability data (273 pairs $\times$ 3 repeats), a single Jev call takes a mean of 1.11s, for a total of 15.2 minutes of compute time across the full corpus -- call latency only, not skill/job profile extraction.

Table~\ref{tab_comparison} shows this system is not a strict win on every axis: Faithfulness and per-answer confidence measure different constructs (groundedness vs.\ decision stability), so neither proves it is ``more accurate.'' But on the axis both systems share -- ranking-order stability -- nothing is traded away: single-call ranking convergence already exceeds the prior tournament-based system's. It also wins on cost -- well under half the calls, no tournament infrastructure -- and offers a native, calibrated confidence the prior system had no way to provide. Its calls also parallelize, unlike the prior system's tournament, where each iteration waits on the last.

As a live, single-job-profile check on end-to-end wall-clock time -- distinct from the corpus-wide call counts above -- both systems were run head-to-head on the frontend-engineer profile against the same 501-CV pool, with candidate skill extraction pre-cached identically for both: the prior system completed shortlisting, assessment, and tournament ranking in 558.7s (8 shortlisted candidates), against 14.2s for Jev (2 shortlisted candidates). The shortlist sizes differ because the two systems' job-profile skill extraction is not identical, not because of the must-have gate (disabling it left the Jev count unchanged), so the fairer statistic is per-candidate cost: 558.7/8 $\approx$ 69.8s for the prior system versus 14.2/2 $\approx$ 7.1s for Jev -- a roughly 6--10$\times$ per-candidate speedup, consistent in direction with the corpus-wide call-count reduction above.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper shows Jev, TypeSafe AI's first System One Model, outperforming the free-text assessment and iterative tournament-ranking stages of an existing pipeline, and validates two adaptations, presented stage by stage in Section III -- an empirical, large-sample test and extension of TypeSafe's own criteria-grounded question design guidance, and cost/latency efficiency from a single parallel pass outperforming the iterative tournament -- each motivated by a reliability problem observed in practice. The result is not a uniform improvement (Table~\ref{tab_comparison}): it trades all free-text interpretability for well under half the model calls, no tournament infrastructure, ranking convergence that already exceeds the prior tournament-based system's (0.964 vs.\ 0.957), and a native calibrated confidence the prior system never had. Criteria-grounded design is strongly validated at scale for the per-requirement Score questions, and for seniority (asked as a single, unfiltered total-years-of-experience judgment, Section III-D) and education alike. As a secondary pipeline detail rather than a third contribution, the pipeline also classifies each job profile's technical skills as must-have or nice-to-have during extraction and gates shortlisting on the must-have subset, its necessity validated with a per-requirement score breakdown.
A limitation is that this work trades away the prior system's free-text interpretability -- a score and a confidence, not a narrative, and per-requirement scores only partly substitute.
Future work includes independently testing RLCD's calibration guarantee against ground-truth correctness rather than self-consistency alone, since calibration does not itself guarantee any individual prediction is correct; testing whether the per-level independence property behind criteria-grounded design holds across TypeSafe's full documented 2--10 level range, not only the five used here; and extending the same criteria-grounded principle to Choice questions, where TypeSafe's own guidance is comparatively undeveloped.
