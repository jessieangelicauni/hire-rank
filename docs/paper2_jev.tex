\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
LLM pipelines for applicant ranking typically combine free-text assessment with iterative listwise comparison, an approach that needs extra machinery against hallucinated claims and provides no built-in confidence signal. In September 2026, TypeSafe AI released Jev, the first ``System One Model'': a non-autoregressive architecture that answers typed questions in one parallel pass and reports a calibrated confidence per answer, removing free-text generation and its hallucination risk by construction. No independent evaluation of this model class exists yet. This study closes that gap by empirically validating and extending vendor-recommended criteria-grounded question design at scale, and by proposing two further architecture-level adaptations for the reliability problems an unmodified Jev introduces: multi-call ensemble averaging and must-have/nice-to-have skill classification for shortlisting correction. It rebuilds an existing LLM-and-tournament applicant-ranking pipeline around Jev, evaluated on 303 shortlisted (job, applicant) pairs across 10 job profiles. The results show that criteria-grounded design raises per-requirement answer confidence from 0.659 to 0.876 and pooled seniority/education confidence from 0.646 to 0.782; that three-call ensemble averaging, run independently and in parallel, recovers ranking convergence from 0.964 to 0.982 -- exceeding the prior tournament-based system's 0.957 -- using well under half as many model calls, with recommendation agreement holding steady across independent repeats. Overall, a non-autoregressive structured-decision model can replace free-text assessment without losing reliability, provided its question design and aggregation match its architecture.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{R}{ecruitment} platforms increasingly use large language models (LLMs) to read resumes, summarize applicant fit, and compare applicants against a job profile, in place of manual screening or keyword search \cite{ref1,ref2,ref3,ref4}. These systems are useful for the same reason they are risky: they generate free text that can assert a strength or weakness the source resume never supports. Prior work mitigates this with prompt-level constraints, retry-on-contradiction loops, post-hoc auditing \cite{ref2}, and iterative listwise tournaments with Plackett-Luce aggregation to turn per-applicant judgments into a defensible ranking \cite{ref1,ref2}. Both exist because next-token sampling over an open vocabulary gives no structural guarantee against an ungrounded claim, and no native signal of the model's confidence.

This paper asks whether answering predefined, typed questions instead of generating open text can replace free-text assessment without reintroducing the reliability problems it is meant to solve. Two gaps motivate this study: first, no independent, peer-reviewed evaluation of System One Models exists yet -- Jev's release \cite{ref11} appeared only days before this paper -- so every reliability claim currently comes from the vendor; second, the recent literature on LLM calibration and self-consistency \cite{ref5,ref6,ref7} targets autoregressive judges that produce a scalar score or a free-text verdict, and none addresses a model whose fundamental outputs are a bounded probability (Noul), a categorical distribution (Choice), or an ordinal distribution (Score) answered in one parallel pass, so its diagnostics and mitigations do not transfer directly.

This paper makes three architecture-level contributions, each validated empirically:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Empirical Validation and Extension of Criteria-Grounded Question Design}: the first large-sample statistical test of TypeSafe's own criteria-grounded Score-question guidance \cite{ref23}, extended to a case the documentation does not cover -- rewriting a seniority question that originally conflated years of experience with domain relevance into a single, criteria-grounded total-years-of-experience match.
\item \textbf{Multi-Call Ensemble Averaging}: combining several independent Jev calls per applicant -- arithmetic mean for score-valued answers, confidence-weighted majority vote for categorical ones -- to match or exceed an iterative tournament's ranking-order stability at well under half the model calls, with no iterative ranking process needed.
\item \textbf{Must-Have/Nice-to-Have Shortlisting Correction}: classifying each job profile's technical skills by importance during extraction and gating shortlisting on the must-have subset, closing a gap that per-requirement score decomposition exposed -- candidates shortlisted almost entirely on peripheral skills.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Literature Review}

The system this paper extends combines LLM-driven free-text assessment with an active listwise tournament aggregated via Bayesian Plackett-Luce under Monte Carlo knowledge-gradient subset selection \cite{ref1,ref2}. Other systems apply LLMs to resume extraction and pointwise scoring inside retrieval-augmented or multi-agent architectures, without a comparative ranking step \cite{ref3,ref4}. None of this work departs from free text or free-form JSON as its output mode, so all of it inherits the hallucination-mitigation burden \cite{ref2} addresses with a retry loop, and none reports a native confidence that could distinguish a well-supported judgment from a guess.

A separate literature asks whether an LLM's stated confidence reflects its true correctness probability. Yaldiz et al.~\cite{ref5} find RL from verifiable rewards improves accuracy but makes decision-making LLMs overconfident. Wang et al.~\cite{ref6} find structured-output reliability varies sharply across models and prompts under a fixed schema. Haldar and Hockenmaier~\cite{ref7} document substantial self-inconsistency in LLM-as-a-judge frameworks, mitigated by averaging judgments; a survey by Gu et al.\ catalogs reliability strategies for such judges, but every one surveyed still targets a free-text or scalar-score judge \cite{ref14}. All of it treats the model as autoregressive, estimating confidence from token probabilities or resampling -- an assumption Jev's natively-trained, non-autoregressive confidence field does not share, so these findings cannot be assumed to transfer. This paper verifies that independently.

Jev is explicitly not a conventional language model: TypeSafe's own documentation states plainly that ``Jev is not a language model. You give it a text and typed questions, and it answers each one with a confidence. It does not write text'' \cite{ref20}. It takes a text state and typed questions (Noul, Choice, or Score) and returns a calibrated probability distribution over each answer in one parallel pass -- no tokens sampled, output priced at zero since none is generated \cite{ref11}. Reported confidence is a derived margin from a decision threshold, not a raw output probability \cite{ref20}. TypeSafe's documentation also recommends writing Score-question criteria as concrete situations rather than bare ordinal labels, illustrated with a single example pair \cite{ref23} -- a claim Section III-B tests at scale rather than taking on the vendor's word.

TypeSafe calls this a ``System One Model,'' trained with a proprietary method it names Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD), optimizing for calibration rather than RLHF's human-preference reward \cite{ref11}; the underlying architecture (encoder-only, decoder-only, or otherwise) is undisclosed in every source consulted. As used here (\texttt{jev-latest}), it accepts up to 64{,}000 tokens per request (32{,}000 for the state plus the longest question) \cite{ref22}.

No paper reviewed here evaluates a System One Model; to the authors' knowledge this is the first independent study of this model class, whose own claims live only in vendor-controlled or community sources at the time of writing \cite{ref11,ref17,ref18,ref19}, none peer-reviewed. This work sits in applicant ranking, already served by a directly comparable free-text predecessor \cite{ref1,ref2}, making an architecture-level comparison possible, not just a benchmark-level one.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Pipeline Overview}
Fig.~\ref{fig_pipeline} shows the end-to-end pipeline, with C1--C3 marking where each contribution intervenes. Its first stage is unchanged from the prior system \cite{ref2}, since it generates no free text: job profiles are loaded as plain text and resumes parsed from PDF. An LLM (Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct) then extracts a normalized skill list including any certification, seniority, and education requirement, additionally classifying each technical skill as must-have or nice-to-have (Section III-D); an applicant is shortlisted only if enough technical skills match by cosine similarity above a fixed threshold and enough of those matches are must-have.

The pipeline changes at the next stage, where the prior system's free text lived: instead of a free-text assessment ranked by an iterative tournament, it sends $n$ independent calls to Jev per shortlisted pair. Each call gives Jev one state (job profile title and description, extracted skills, resume text) and typed questions answered in one pass: a hiring-recommendation Choice, a minimum-qualifications Noul, one Score per technical requirement, one Score per stated seniority or education requirement, and one Noul per stated certification. Every Score question offers a fixed set of ordinal levels (Section III-B), linearly mapped to 0--100 for reporting -- this range is the mapping's resolution, not a continuous per-call judgment. A composite fit score is then computed, not asked: the unweighted mean of every technical-requirement, seniority, and education Score in the call, a transparent and auditable aggregate rather than an independent Jev judgment. The $n$ calls are combined (Section III-C), applicants ranked by the combined composite score, and a diagnostic check (Section III-D) reads per-requirement scores afterward for upstream errors. Skill/job profile extraction runs on a conventional autoregressive LLM, unlike Jev.

\subsection{Criteria-Grounded Question Design}
Match-degree questions -- technical requirements, seniority, and education -- use Score, not Choice: TypeSafe reserves Choice for unordered categories and Score for a position on a spectrum \cite{ref23,ref24}, exactly what a match degree is.

An initial implementation labeled every Score question's five levels with bare numbers (``0,'' ``25,'' ``50,'' ``75,'' ``100'') and no description of what each meant. With no stated meaning to match, Jev had to guess the boundary, and guessing lowers confidence. The ambiguity was in the labels, not the primitive: an equally undescribed Choice (e.g., unlabeled \texttt{low}/\texttt{medium}/\texttt{high}) would be just as unclear. The fix, following TypeSafe's own recommendation \cite{ref23}, describes each level as a concrete situation instead: e.g., a requirement ``mentioned only as a bare skill-list item'' versus one ``used substantively in a real role or project with clear responsibility.'' The model then matches evidence to a description instead of guessing.

To check that this fix, not sampling noise, caused the confidence gain, a paired ablation compared the two designs on identical input: for a sampled subset of already-assessed pairs, three independent fresh calls were issued against the same state under the original bare-label design and averaged the same way as production (Section III-C), and confidence compared pairwise. Both sides are therefore a mean across 3 calls -- the concrete side already recorded in production, the bare-label side freshly collected -- so neither side carries a variance advantage that could manufacture the gain reported. The primary comparison is the per-technical-requirement questions; a Wilcoxon signed-rank test with rank-biserial effect size was applied.

The same fix generalizes to the education Score question, which had the same bare-label problem: field and degree-level match are rewritten as concrete evidence, tested with a second paired ablation on all pairs whose job profile states an education requirement. The seniority question had a different problem beyond bare labels: an initial design asked Jev to judge years of experience and domain relevance in one ordinal answer, conflating an objective count with a subjective fit judgment -- asking for years of \emph{relevant} experience silently required doing both at once. The final design instead asks a single, unfiltered question, evaluated only when the job profile states an explicit minimum (extracted as \texttt{seniority\_min\_years} during skill extraction): how many total years of experience does the CV show, independent of domain, matched against a literal years threshold. Domain relevance is not asked as a separate question -- it is already captured through the per-technical-requirement Score questions the composite fit score aggregates (Section III-A). Generating levels per job profile with an LLM was rejected throughout: it reintroduces free-text generation and makes scores incomparable across job profiles.

\subsection{Multi-Call Ensemble Averaging}
A single Jev call is a single sample, carrying noise that can shift a ranking. Since calls are independent and cheap, the pipeline issues $n$ calls per pair -- three by default, matching \cite{ref2}'s stability-repeat count -- combined per field: numeric fields by arithmetic mean, the recommendation by majority vote (ties toward higher mean confidence), and binary answers by simple majority, defaulting to the conservative (negative) outcome on a tie.

Averaging could also hide real disagreement rather than remove noise: ranking convergence was measured both from single, unaggregated calls (Kendall-$\tau$ across three per-pair rankings) and from three three-call averages. If averaging only hid disagreement, the second would not consistently beat the first.

\subsection{Must-Have/Nice-to-Have Shortlisting Correction}
Because Jev reports a separate score per extracted requirement rather than a single free-text verdict, a job profile's shortlisted pool can be inspected at the level of individual skills, not only an aggregate score. This granularity is used diagnostically: a requirement is informative only if the pool's median score on it is reasonably high; an applicant scoring near-zero on most informative requirements, despite being shortlisted, is flagged -- not because Jev judged them harshly, but because peers could clear a bar this applicant could not. Applied to the corpus, this diagnostic surfaced a concrete defect: on the cloud-engineer profile, 48\% of shortlisted candidates scored near-zero on the profile's only informative core requirement (cost optimization), despite having passed the prior shortlisting rule's total skill-match threshold -- meaning they were admitted almost entirely on peripheral, nice-to-have skills rather than the requirements that actually define the role.

This motivates a direct correction at the source: during skill extraction (Section III-A), each job profile's technical skills are also classified as must-have or nice-to-have -- the small core the role is built around versus general-purpose tooling and infrastructure that supports but does not define it. Shortlisting then requires a candidate to match a minimum number of must-have skills, not only a minimum total.

\section{Experimental Setup}
The pipeline is evaluated on the prior system's corpus and shortlist configuration \cite{ref2}, with every setting held fixed across evaluations so the assessment-stage change is the one independent variable between the two systems; Table~\ref{tab_config} shows the full configuration. Three evaluation procedures are applied: repeatability (three independent single calls per pair); ranking convergence (Kendall-$\tau$, Section III-C, from single calls and three-call averages); and the criteria-design ablations of Section III-B. Spearman is used wherever the size of a score gap carries meaning, such as the per-requirement score diagnostic of Section III-D; Kendall-$\tau$ is used only for ranking convergence, where relative candidate order is what matters, not the magnitude of any score. All tests are two-sided except where a directional hypothesis is stated explicitly.

\subsection{Must-Have/Nice-to-Have Shortlisting Correction}
Applied to the full corpus, the must-have gate (Section III-D) excludes a meaningful share of candidates who would have passed the prior total-only rule: 44 of 303 previously-shortlisted candidates (14.5\%) are excluded. The gate is driven by classifying each job profile's technical skills as must-have or nice-to-have during extraction, of which 39\% were classified must-have.

Repeating the repeatability and ranking-convergence evaluation on this must-have-filtered pool shows shortlisting correction and the rewritten seniority question do not degrade Jev's core reliability properties: recommendation agreement across repeats remains comparable, composite-score repeat stdev is essentially unchanged, and single-call ranking convergence is only slightly lower -- consistent with restriction of range: excluding candidates on peripheral-skill grounds leaves a more uniformly-qualified pool, in which similarly-qualified candidates are intrinsically harder to rank apart consistently. The criteria-grounding findings hold, reported in Table~\ref{tab_score_ablation} above, which is itself computed on this same must-have-filtered pool.

\subsection{Efficiency and Comparison with the Prior Tournament-Based System}
Across the repeatability data (303 pairs $\times$ 3 repeats), a single Jev call takes a mean of 1.12s, for a total of 17.0 minutes of compute time across the full corpus -- call latency only, not skill/job profile extraction.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper replaced the free-text assessment and iterative tournament-ranking stages of an existing pipeline with Jev, TypeSafe AI's first System One Model, and validated three adaptations -- an empirical, large-sample test and extension of TypeSafe's own criteria-grounded question design guidance, multi-call ensemble averaging, and must-have/nice-to-have skill classification for shortlisting correction -- each motivated by a reliability problem observed in practice. The result is not a uniform improvement (Table~\ref{tab_comparison}): it trades all free-text interpretability for well under half the model calls, no tournament infrastructure, ranking convergence that now exceeds the prior tournament-based system's (0.982 vs.\ 0.957), and a native calibrated confidence the prior system never had. Criteria-grounded design is strongly validated at scale for the per-requirement Score questions and, once the seniority question is rewritten as a single, unfiltered total-years-of-experience judgment (Section III-B), for seniority and education alike. This paper also corrects a shortlisting-stage defect, found using per-requirement score decomposition, by classifying each job profile's technical skills as must-have or nice-to-have during extraction and gating shortlisting on the must-have subset.
A limitation is that this work trades away the prior system's free-text interpretability -- a score and a confidence, not a narrative, and per-requirement scores only partly substitute. A second is that the must-have/nice-to-have classification (Section III-D) is itself an unvalidated LLM judgment made once during skill extraction, with no ground truth to confirm it matches what a hiring manager would actually intend.
Future work includes a lightweight explanation layer -- surfacing the lowest-scoring items as a structured rationale -- to partially restore interpretability without reintroducing free text; and validating the must-have/nice-to-have classification itself, e.g., against human-labeled job profiles, to quantify how often it misclassifies a peripheral skill as core.
