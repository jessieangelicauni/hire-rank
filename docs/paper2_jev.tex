\begin{abstract}
LLM pipelines for applicant ranking combine free-text assessment with iterative listwise comparison, needing extra hallucination-mitigation machinery and no confidence signal. In September 2026, TypeSafe AI released Jev, the first ``System One Model,'' a non-autoregressive architecture answering typed questions in one parallel pass with calibrated per-answer confidence, removing free-text hallucination and schema violation by design. This paper aims to deploy Jev in a real applicant-ranking pipeline through three contributions: criteria-grounded question design, single-pass cost/latency efficiency over an iterative tournament, and repeat-to-repeat reliability testing. The research method is transforming an existing LLM-and-tournament pipeline into a fully structured typed-question architecture, evaluated on 273 shortlisted (job, applicant) pairs across 10 job profiles against the closest prior system. Criteria-grounded design raises per-requirement confidence from 0.672 to 0.894 and pooled seniority/education confidence from 0.635 to 0.766. The pipeline needs under half as many model calls (273 versus roughly 2,061), shows lower per-candidate latency (16.5s versus 74.3s), zero-priced output, and confidence of 0.894 versus the prior system's Faithfulness of 0.880. Repeat-to-repeat tests show ranking stability matching the prior system (Kendall's $\tau=0.965$ versus $\approx$0.957). A non-autoregressive structured-decision model can equal free-text assessment reliability at lower cost and latency, given matching question design, efficiency, and stability.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{R}{ecruitment} platforms increasingly use large language models (LLMs) to read resumes, summarize applicant fit, and compare applicants against a job profile, in place of manual screening or keyword search \cite{ref1,ref2,ref3,ref4}. These systems are useful for the same reason they are risky: they generate free text that can assert a strength or weakness the source resume never supports. Prior work mitigates this with prompt-level constraints, retry-on-contradiction loops, and post-hoc groundedness auditing \cite{ref2}, alongside iterative listwise tournaments with Plackett-Luce aggregation to turn per-applicant judgments into a defensible ranking \cite{ref1,ref2}. Both exist because next-token sampling over an open vocabulary gives no structural guarantee against an ungrounded claim, and no native signal of the model's confidence.

This paper asks whether answering predefined, typed questions instead of generating open text can outperform free-text assessment without reintroducing the reliability problems it is meant to solve. Two gaps motivate this study: first, no independent, peer-reviewed evaluation of System One Models exists yet: every reliability claim currently comes from TypeSafe-controlled or community sources \cite{ref5,ref6,ref7,ref8,ref9}; second, the recent literature on LLM calibration and self-consistency \cite{ref10,ref11,ref12,ref13} targets autoregressive judges that produce a scalar score or a free-text verdict, and none addresses a model whose fundamental outputs are a bounded probability (Noul), a categorical distribution (Choice), or an ordinal distribution (Score) answered in one parallel pass, so its tests and mitigations do not transfer directly.

Beyond the primitive specifications, TypeSafe's documentation and the cited community sources provide implementation guidance largely absent from the academic literature on structured-output LLMs, including how to decompose a multi-factor judgment into separate fields and aggregate them deterministically (Section III) \cite{ref6,ref7}.
This paper makes the following three architecture-level contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Criteria-Grounded Question Design}: the first large-sample statistical test of TypeSafe's own criteria-grounded Score-question guidance, extended to seniority and education Score questions, each validated with concrete-situation criteria in place of bare ordinal labels.
\item \textbf{Cost and Latency Efficiency}: a direct sort on Jev's per-call overall score outperforming the prior system's iterative listwise tournament -- no iterative ranking process, all calls parallelize, total model calls cut well below the prior system's, and per-candidate wall-clock latency substantially lower.
\item \textbf{Reliability Tests}: repeat-to-repeat evidence that Jev's single-call architecture is stable enough to support a direct-sort ranking, with no iterative or ensemble correction -- high ranking stability and high repeat reliability across three independent repeats (ICC$=0.998$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Literature Review}

The system this paper extends combines LLM-driven free-text assessment with an active listwise tournament aggregated via Bayesian Plackett-Luce under Monte Carlo knowledge-gradient subset selection, which its authors report yields rankings that are globally coherent and sample-efficient \cite{ref1,ref2}. Other systems apply LLMs to resume extraction and pointwise scoring inside retrieval-augmented or multi-agent architectures, without a comparative ranking step: a RAG-based recruitment support system reports strong retrieval-faithfulness metrics on its test set \cite{ref3}, and a multi-agent RAG-LLM resume screener evaluated against HR-professional ratings on anonymized resumes reports its findings as demonstrating the potential of such systems for scalable hiring workflows \cite{ref4}, alongside a transformer-based O*NET job-matching approach reported to outperform its baselines in both evaluated scenarios \cite{ref14}. Most of this work does not depart from free text or free-form JSON as its output mode, so it inherits the hallucination-mitigation burden \cite{ref2} addresses with a retry loop -- a burden also targeted by non-generative hallucination detectors \cite{ref15}. The O*NET job-matching approach \cite{ref14} avoids that burden by scoring similarity directly from embeddings rather than generating text, but like the rest reports no native confidence that could distinguish a well-supported judgment from a guess.

A separate literature asks whether an LLM's stated confidence reflects its true correctness probability. Yaldiz et al.~\cite{ref10} find RL from verifiable rewards (RLVR) improves accuracy but makes decision-making LLMs overconfident. Wang et al.~\cite{ref11} find structured-output reliability varies sharply across models and prompts under a fixed schema. Haldar and Hockenmaier~\cite{ref12} document substantial self-inconsistency in LLM-as-a-judge frameworks, mitigated by averaging judgments; a survey by Gu et al.\ covers reliability strategies for such judges, but every one surveyed still targets a free-text or scalar-score judge \cite{ref16}. All of it treats the model as autoregressive, estimating confidence from token probabilities or resampling -- an assumption Jev's natively-trained, non-autoregressive confidence field does not share, so these findings cannot be assumed to transfer.

Jev is explicitly not a conventional language model: TypeSafe's own documentation states plainly that ``Jev is not a language model. You give it a text and typed questions, and it answers each one with a confidence. It does not write text'' \cite{ref9}. Mechanically, it replaces autoregressive sampling with a parallel sampler: for every typed question, the possible outputs are enumerated in advance and scored in one pass over the state, so the output always matches the expected format by design, rather than being checked and fixed after generation -- no tokens are generated, and output is priced at zero accordingly \cite{ref5}. TypeSafe reports this at \$0.042 per million input tokens, versus \$0.20--\$10 per million input tokens for conventional autoregressive APIs where output is typically priced roughly 5$\times$ higher than input, and a 70--500ms end-to-end response time versus 3--329 seconds for frontier models -- a claimed 40--200$\times$ speedup on comparably-shaped queries \cite{ref5}. Every question in a call is answered independently against the same state, so adding a question to a call costs negligibly more than the call alone \cite{ref5}.

This paper's typed questions all use the \emph{Score} primitive, which supports 2--10 ordinal levels and returns a probability-weighted mean position; its confidence is a margin from a decision threshold, not a raw output probability \cite{ref9}. Each level is scored independently -- the model never sees the level's number or its neighbors -- so a level labeled only by a number (e.g., ``50'' vs.\ ``75'') gives the model nothing to act on, while a level described as a concrete situation gives it resume evidence to match against. This is why TypeSafe recommends writing Score-question criteria as concrete situations.

TypeSafe calls this a ``System One Model,'' trained with a proprietary method it names Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD), which optimizes for calibration directly rather than for a human-preference reward (RLHF) or a programmatically verifiable outcome reward (RLVR -- the paradigm Yaldiz et al.\ \cite{ref10} show becomes overconfident even as it grows more accurate) \cite{ref5}. Calibration is a narrower guarantee than correctness: as TypeSafe's own community documentation puts it, ``calibration does not mean that an individual prediction is guaranteed to be correct'' \cite{ref7} -- it means reported confidence tracks the true hit rate across many predictions, which is what the confidence-based tests used throughout this pipeline rely on.

Figure~\ref{fig_pipeline} shows the end-to-end pipeline, described in turn below: criteria-grounded questions (Criteria-Grounded Question Design) feed the Jev inference step, and deterministic aggregation and direct-sort ranking (Cost and Latency Efficiency) turn its structured outputs into a final ranking. Extraction and shortlisting are unchanged in kind from \emph{Hallucination-Aware Self-Correction for LLM-Based Applicant Assessment} \cite{ref2}, since neither generates free text. The pipeline diverges from the assessment step onward, where typed calls to Jev are issued once in parallel per shortlisted pair and a direct sort replaces the prior system's free-text assessment and iterative tournament, most directly benefiting cost and latency.

\subsection{Ingestion and Skill/Job Profile Extraction}
Job profiles and resumes are ingested as plain text, and an autoregressive LLM (Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct) extracts a normalized skill list, each technical skill additionally classified as must-have or nice-to-have for use by shortlisting. This step does not use Jev and is not one of this paper's three contributions; its output feeds the assessment step.

\subsection{Shortlisting}
An applicant is shortlisted only if at least 5 extracted skills match the job profile's by cosine similarity $\geq$0.8, and at least 2 of those matches are must-have skills (auto-reduced to the job profile's must-have count if smaller); requiring must-have coverage, not only a total count, keeps a shortlisted candidate from being admitted almost entirely on nice-to-have skills. Shortlisting, like extraction, does not use Jev.

\subsection{Structured Assessment via Jev (Criteria-Grounded Question Design)}
Where the prior system's free text lived, the pipeline sends one call to Jev per shortlisted pair, all issued in parallel with no dependency between pairs. Each call gives Jev one state (job profile, extracted skills, resume text) and typed Score questions answered in one pass: one Score per technical requirement, and one Score per stated seniority or education requirement -- one judgment per field, combined afterward.
Match-degree questions use Score, not Choice, since match degree is a spectrum position. Each Score question's five levels are written as concrete situations, not bare ordinal labels, exploiting the per-level independence from Section II: e.g., ``mentioned only as a bare skill-list item'' versus ``used substantively in a real role or project with clear responsibility.'' Education follows the same design. The seniority question -- asked only when the job profile states an explicit minimum -- is a single, objective years-of-experience match, kept separate from the subjective per-requirement judgment. Section V validates this design at scale against a bare ordinal-number (0/25/50/75/100) baseline.

An overall fit score is then computed following TypeSafe's documented aggregation pattern described earlier: each Score normalized to its $[0,1]$ position, and the overall score is the unweighted mean of every technical-requirement, seniority, and education Score in the call.

\subsection{Ranking}
The assessment step yields one overall score per (job, applicant) pair; applicants are ranked by a direct sort on this score. This direct sort outperforms the prior system's iterative Monte Carlo knowledge-gradient tournament with Bayesian Plackett-Luce aggregation: no ranking process, no waiting on a prior iteration's result. Because Jev's calls need no iterative dependency, they also parallelize, unlike the prior system's tournament, quantified later in the results as a reduction in model calls and wall-clock latency. Repeating that assessment call three times per pair, independently, measures how stable the resulting ranking is to repetition (Reliability Tests), also reported later in the results.

\section{Experimental Setup}
The pipeline is evaluated on the corpus and shortlist configuration of \emph{Hallucination-Aware Self-Correction for LLM-Based Applicant Assessment} \cite{ref2}, with every setting held fixed across evaluations so the assessment-stage change is the one independent variable between the two systems. The 500 resumes are sampled from the EraMatch CV Parsing Benchmark v3.0 synthetic resume dataset \cite{ref17}, matching that same corpus \cite{ref2}. The 10 job profiles are synthetic, authored by the paper's own authors \cite{ref2}.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Four metrics drive the comparisons in Section V. \textbf{Calibrated confidence} is Jev's native per-answer signal, averaged across answers; it is this paper's counterpart to the prior system's separately-computed Faithfulness score, though the two measure different constructs (confidence calibration vs.\ groundedness) and are not on a shared scale. \textbf{ICC(1,1)}, a one-way random-effects intraclass correlation, measures what fraction of score variance across repeated calls is real between-applicant signal rather than repeat noise. \textbf{Kendall's $\tau$} measures rank-order agreement between two rankings of the same applicants, used both for repeat-to-repeat ranking stability and for comparison against the prior system's own reported value. \textbf{Wilcoxon signed-rank test}, with rank-biserial effect size $r$, tests whether criteria-grounded question design significantly raises confidence over a bare ordinal-number baseline.

\subsection{Criteria-Grounded Question Design}

Figure~\ref{fig_score_ablation} shows each question asked twice under the two criteria designs, with mean confidence under each. All differences are significant by Wilcoxon signed-rank test ($p<0.001$), with a large rank-biserial effect size and a 95\% bootstrap confidence interval (10,000 resamples) on the mean confidence gain for per-requirement ($n=772$, $r=0.89$, gain $0.223$, 95\% CI $[0.204, 0.241]$) and seniority ($n=40$, $r=0.77$, gain $0.254$, 95\% CI $[0.163, 0.336]$) questions, and a medium-to-large effect for education and pooled seniority/education questions ($n=116$ and $156$, $r=0.47$--$0.58$, gain $0.090$ and $0.132$, 95\% CI $[0.041, 0.137]$ and $[0.088, 0.176]$). The gain is not specific to per-requirement questions.

\subsection{Must-Have Gate: Test Validation}
Applied to the full corpus, the must-have gate excludes 32 of 305 candidates (10.5\%) that a total-only baseline would shortlist: candidates who matched enough skills in total but not enough must-have skills. The gate is driven by classifying each job profile's technical skills as must-have or nice-to-have during extraction, of which 46.1\% were classified must-have. This gate is a pipeline design validated using Criteria-Grounded Question Design's per-requirement detail, not one of this paper's three architecture-level contributions.

The criteria-grounding findings hold on this same must-have-gated pool, already reflected in Figure~\ref{fig_score_ablation}.

\subsection{Reliability Tests}
These tests measure Jev's repeat-to-repeat decision stability, using the same repeated calls as the latency measurements above: they characterize how consistent a single call is with itself, not a variable this paper manipulates.

\textbf{Overall-score and per-requirement stability.} Across the three repeats, the overall fit score varies little per pair, and is noticeably more stable than the individual per-technical-requirement scores that feed it, so averaging across the requirements in a call cancels most of the per-question noise before ranking ever sees it.

\textbf{Repeat reliability (ICC).} ICC(1,1) measures what fraction of the score variance across the three repeats comes from real differences between applicants, rather than from noise in repeating the same call. Computed per job profile from the three repeats' overall fit scores, the corpus mean is ICC$=0.998$: almost all of the variance is applicant signal, not repeat noise.

\textbf{Ranking stability.} While the repeat reliability results show the repeats' scores agree, ranking stability tests whether the resulting applicant order does too: for each job profile, every pair of the three repeats' rankings is compared by Kendall's $\tau$, then averaged across pairs and profiles.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper compares Jev, TypeSafe AI's first System One Model, against the authors' own \emph{Hallucination-Aware Self-Correction for LLM-Based Applicant Assessment} \cite{ref2} -- an LLM-driven (Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct) free-text assessment with an iterative Monte Carlo knowledge-gradient tournament and Bayesian Plackett-Luce ranking -- through three contributions: an empirical, large-sample test and extension of TypeSafe's own criteria-grounded question design guidance; cost/latency efficiency from a single parallel pass outperforming the iterative tournament; and repeat-to-repeat reliability tests of that single-call architecture. Against that prior system, this pipeline outperforms on cost and latency: well under half the model calls (273 versus $\approx$2,061) and, on a single live head-to-head profile comparison, lower per-candidate latency (16.5s versus 74.3s), with output priced at zero by design. It also removes the specific risk of free-text hallucination and schema violation by design (an individual structured judgment can still be wrong), and offers a native calibrated confidence the prior system could not provide -- though the two systems' confidence signals are not on a directly comparable scale (Section III). Criteria-grounded design raises answer confidence at scale, and reliability tests show ranking stability at least matching the prior system's (Kendall's $\tau=0.965$ versus $\approx$0.957) -- not a clear win, but no regression from a substantially cheaper architecture.
A limitation is that this work trades away the prior system's free-text interpretability -- a score and a confidence, not a narrative, and per-requirement scores only partly substitute.
This system is a screening aid, not an automated hire/reject decision: any adverse action should involve human review of the underlying score breakdown and resume evidence, not an unexamined rank cutoff. Calibrated confidence -- confidence that tracks the empirical hit rate across predictions (Section II) -- is not a fairness or non-discrimination guarantee, and a well-calibrated model can still be systematically biased against a protected group.
Future work includes independently testing RLCD's calibration guarantee against ground-truth correctness rather than self-consistency alone, since calibration does not itself guarantee any individual prediction is correct.
